Observers

An observer is a first-class thing: something that can honestly say "I saw." On a record it's a stable lineage id. Names, keys, grants and reliability are all records about it, relative to whoever states them. Names never take part in matching.

Three concerns that got mixed together

Naming

Who is this?

The name on every record. Answered by naming systems: Kerberos, SDSI, petnames, DIDs.

Key binding

Which keys speak for this name?

Records about the observer. A parent vouches for a child's key.

Authority

What may it do, and for whom?

A store's admission policy over endorsement records. Your OBO and endorsements work lives here.

Today one .vertex observers {} block is the allow-list, the grant and the key registry at once. Most of the CLI and SDK drift comes from that.

Name anatomy

  • kyle/safari, kyle/rss: my instruments, observing on my behalf
  • kyle/claude: an agent working with me; kyle/claude/reviewer is its sub-agent
  • alex@acme: disambiguated once stores compose across people

On the wire, and what each layer does with names
{"kind":"obs", "observer":"sl:7f3a…", "emitted":"2026-10-17T14:02:11Z", …}
{"kind":"name", "observer":"sl:a1c0…", "about":["sl:7f3a…"], "name":"claude"}    ← kyle's name for it
  • Folds and admission compare ids. Equality replaces today's leaf-tolerant matching, and the CLI-warns vs SDK-raises split disappears.
  • Lenses map ids back to names, using the reader's own name records first. Your view can say kyle/claude while Sam's says team/reviewer-bot; both are right.
  • Input (CLI, queries, specs) resolves names to ids before anything is stored. Ambiguity is an error at typing time, never in storage.
  • / is naming only. kyle/claude grants nothing; that's a grant record. Naming a child and vouching for its key often happen together, but stay separate records.
  • Today's ObserverDecl(name, identity, grant, key) splits into four records: name, lineage id, grant, key binding. The unused identity field suggests that split was already wanted.
  • Zero-config start: the first emit in an empty store makes a local lineage named from the environment (e.g. $USER).

Delegation, separated

Lean: an observation carries one optional pointer, under: <grant hash>. It's a reference, not the delegation itself, so the admission fold checks it directly and readers who don't care ignore it. The grant, its scope and its revocation stay separate records. This sits between AT Protocol (no delegation in records at all) and Nostr NIP-26 (a signed delegation inside every event).

Does AT Protocol do delegation?

Not in the sense needed here. It delegates access to an account, not one identity acting for another. Records carry no actor-vs-principal distinction: a post written by an OAuth app looks exactly like one you wrote.

The gap: no chain from actor to principal. A third-party server, Tranquil PDS, has an open pull request adding controllers, role presets and an RFC 8693-style act claim: people reaching for exactly the missing piece, at the token layer rather than in records.

Where the missing piece lives
  • KERI delegated identifiers: closest structural match to lineage = hash(anchor). A delegate's creation (and, as I understand it, its rotations) takes effect only once the delegator anchors a seal of it in its own event log. Cooperative: the parent vouches but never holds the child's keys.
  • Matrix cross-signing: master key signs a self-signing key, which signs each device. "Parent vouches for child" in production at scale.
  • RFC 8693 nested act: the shape for "C acting for B acting for A" when more than one hop must be visible.
  • UCAN, Biscuit: attenuation at each hop, checkable offline.

Authority as a vertex

Where receipt time still matters. emitted is self-stamped, so a compromised key can backdate a record to before its revocation. The defense is evidence that the record existed by some time: a store's receipt, or a tick that covered it (as in Certificate Transparency or RFC 3161 timestamping). That's the one real job "witnessed by a store" does. It belongs as a narrow tool in the sharing layer, not in the foundation.

How observers work in the code today
  • A free-form string with no grammar. The only structure is / namespacing (principal/agent). Default kyle/loops-claude via LOOPS_OBSERVER. No @ convention exists.
  • Fact: observer: str plus origin. Documented as required, but __init__ defaults to "". Only arrival rejects an empty observer.
  • Tick: no observer field (this fits). But re-entering ticks get the vertex name as observer, and arrival added a custodian (the vertex stem, also a privileged "self-observer") to fill the slot.
  • Declarations: ObserverDecl(name, identity, grant, key). The identity field is parsed but never read.
  • Keys: registry keyed by observer name; private keys under keys/<observer>/ed25519.key. Arrival keeps its own in-log key registry.
  • Admission: no block means unrestricted; undeclared means UnknownObserver in the SDK, but only a warning in the CLI.

Inconsistencies found

  • Two matching rules: the CLI is leaf-tolerant and the SDK is exact. kyle/loops-claude passes sl emit but raises UnknownObserver through the SDK.
  • The focus.X rule enforces meaning at write (observer == X). Under the model it's hearsay, and a fold can filter at read.
  • The custodian overlaps the self-observer. TickWindow.observer is really the boundary name.

Prior art: naming without a central registry
  • Zooko's triangle (about 2001): a name can be human-meaningful, secure and decentralized, but only two at once.
  • Petname systems (Marc Stiegler, 2005): a key (global, secure, unreadable), a nickname (self-claimed, readable, not unique), a petname (my name for you). Your point that observations about observers are relative is a petname system.
  • SDSI (Rivest and Lampson, 1996; merged into SPKI, RFC 2693): every principal has a local namespace and names link across them. There are no global names. Closest fit: kyle/claude is "the principal kyle calls claude", and kyle vouches for its key.
  • Secure Scuttlebutt: an identity is an Ed25519 key with its own single-writer signed feed; names are about messages, including names for others. Very close to sl. Its lessons: identity = key meant a lost key was a lost identity, and one feed per key made multiple devices painful.
  • Keybase sigchains: a signed, append-only chain of statements a user makes about themselves (keys, devices, proofs). A concrete model of an observer's self-description as its own small store.
  • W3C DIDs: did:method:id resolving to a document of keys with rotation. did:key is self-certifying, did:web is anchored in DNS.
Prior art: delegation and authority
  • OAuth Token Exchange, RFC 8693: sub is the principal, act is the actor; nested act records a delegation chain per token. RFC 9396 (Rich Authorization Requests) adds authorization_details.
  • Attenuating capabilities, where you can only pass on less than you hold: SPKI authorization certificates, Macaroons (caveats chained with HMAC), Biscuit (public-key, Datalog, offline attenuation), UCAN (DID-based proof chains). Your endorsements belong to this family. Its useful checks are enforced attenuation, offline verification and revocation.
  • Nostr NIP-26 (delegated event signing) put delegation into the event envelope and has since been marked unrecommended. Every client had to understand it, for little gain. The lesson is to keep authority out of the record envelope.

Question this raised, now leaning

Is delegation part of the name or part of each observation? A Kerberos- or SDSI-style name is static, so a bot acting for both you and Alex needs two names. An RFC 8693-style per-record reference is dynamic. Lean: the name says who named whom; a grant record says on whose behalf; an observation points at its grant with under.
